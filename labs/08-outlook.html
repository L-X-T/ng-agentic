From Workshop to Everyday Workflow

The closing lab. No new tooling, no new technique – this one turns seven modules of machinery into decisions: did the setup move the goal you wrote before the workshop, what do you adopt first, and what will you let it cost? You leave with a written plan, not good intentions.

About this lab

This is Lab 08 – the send-off. In Lab 00 you wrote a project brief and a goal before any of the tooling existed; every lab since has been building the machinery to reach it. Now you close that loop and open the next one: everything in this lab is writing, not coding – three short artifacts that decide whether the workshop survives contact with Monday. The guide behind it is 08-OUTLOOK.md.

Why this matters

Workshops fade; defaults stick. A plan written in the room – with names, paths, and a budget – is what turns "we should use agents more" into a convention in your AGENTS.md next week. The horizon will keep moving (better models, shorter instruction files, longer leashes); what you write now decides how your setup moves with it.

Learning objectives

  • Hold your workshop project against the Lab 00 goal – and name what actually blocked the stretch.
  • Turn the workshop into an adoption plan with four concrete firsts.
  • Decide a model & budget strategy: pricing shape, a configuration per task type, and a spend cap for autonomy.
  • Bonus: sketch how the setup rolls out to a whole team – ownership, sharing, and PR etiquette.

Prerequisites

  • Labs 00–07 done (or at least 00–04 plus one workflow lab).
  • Your Lab 00 brief and the required goal written before the exercises.
  • Somewhere durable to write: a docs/ note in your repo, your team wiki – not a scratchpad you'll lose.

1 Look back – your Lab 00 brief and goal

Goal: measure the workshop against the target you set before it started – honestly.

Before any agent touched your project, you wrote a one-paragraph brief (Lab 00, step 3) and a mandatory goal to aim for. That was the point of writing them down: they are the only measurement of this workshop that isn't hindsight. If the original goal is missing, record that limitation. Do not reconstruct a supposed before-class goal from memory.

Your task

Reopen your brief and goal, and answer three questions in writing:

  1. Did you reach the realistic goal? If not, how far did you get?
  2. If you wrote a stretch goal, what blocked it – missing verification (tests, checks), an unclear spec, a setup gap, or genuinely the model? If you did not, what would the next step beyond the realistic goal have needed?
  3. Which lab moved your project most – and which one will you re-read first?
Show solution – an example reflection

Continuing Lab 00's recipe box example:

  • Realistic goal ("agent builds list + detail following conventions") – reached in Lab 05: the feature workflow (spec → plan → implement) produced both views, on-style, lint-clean.
  • Stretch goal ("accessible, tested app with search and tagging") – blocked on verification: without e2e coverage there was no safety net for a Lab 06 autonomous run at search, so it stayed manual. Not a model problem – a checks problem.
  • Biggest mover – Lab 01: once AGENTS.md and the linter carried the conventions, every later prompt got shorter.

Use the evidence from your run to identify the blocker. Missing checks, model capability, time and scope can each be real constraints.

Checkpoint: three written answers, and the gap beyond the realistic goal named as a specific gap (a missing check, an unwritten spec, a setup hole) – include time and scope when they actually limited the result.

2 Your adoption plan – four firsts

Goal: pick the first concrete instance of each workshop practice for your project – small enough to happen this week.

Everything you used here lives in this repo; nothing transfers by itself. The transfer mechanism is picking one small, named first for each practice – a convention, a skill, an autonomous task, a CI hook – and doing it while the workshop is fresh.

Your task

Write four lines, each naming one concrete first (the adoption sheet's first-week table has a row for each):

  1. First convention to promote into your own AGENTS.md – then validate it with Lab 01's validation loop, don't just trust it.
  2. First skill to port from .agents/skills/ – or to write, for a process your team actually repeats.
  3. First task you'd trust to a goal-driven run – it must pass the Lab 06 guide's contract: verifiable goal, feedback signal, guardrails.
  4. First CI or PR-bot candidate – one check or babysitting duty an agent could own in your pipeline.

Notice the shape: every entry is deliberately small and names a file, a branch, or a suite. A plan you could finish in one sitting is a plan that gets finished.

Checkpoint: four firsts written down, each naming a concrete target (a rule, a skill, a task, a pipeline job) – categories like "improve testing" don't count.

3 Your model & budget strategy

Goal: decide how you'll pay, which model does which work, and where the cap sits – before the first invoice decides for you.

Use the chapter’s comparison method: choose configurations from observed task results and risk, then account for usage, review and rework. Keep unknown costs explicit.

Your task

Write your strategy as three decisions:

  1. Pricing shape – subscription, metered API, or both? If both: who runs on the subscription, and what runs on the meter?
  2. Configuration per task type – compare two configurations on fixed acceptance cases and record correctness, review time and repairs before selecting a default.
  3. The cap – a spend limit for everything autonomous (loops, orchestrators, bots), and where it's enforced: an enforced provider/runner limit or a notification-only alert; label the difference.
Show solution – an example strategy
WorkPricingModel tier
Interactive daily work – prompting, grilling, HITL workflowsSubscriptionStrong (it's what you talk to all day)
Mechanical changes, assessed for impactMeteredMid tier by default: our two-run comparison showed equal acceptance at a third of the review time; escalate when the impact is high
Planning, review and implementationEitherStrongest tier: fewer repairs and shorter reviews in the comparison outweighed the higher run cost
CI jobs, PR bots, always-on agentsMetered with a monthly cap + alertMatch to the duty; cap regardless

Measure useful accepted outcomes and the human work required to judge them. A correct diagnosis or justified rejection can be valuable without a merged diff.

Caps before climbing

Set the spend cap before your first unattended run, not after the first surprising invoice. It belongs to Lab 06's guardrails, right next to "no push" and "no prod credentials".

Checkpoint: three decisions written down – pricing shape, a configuration per task type, and a numeric cap with an enforcement point for anything autonomous.

4 Sketch the team rollout · Bonus

Goal: decide how the harness becomes the team's harness – ownership, sharing, and etiquette.

One person with a harness is faster; a team sharing one compounds. The setup was built for that – it lives in the repo, not in your head – but sharing it takes four explicit agreements.

Your task

Sketch answers to four questions:

  1. Who owns AGENTS.md and the style guide? They're shared contracts – changed via PR, reviewed like code. Who merges?
  2. How do skills get shared and reviewed? Committed under .agents/skills/ so improvements reach everyone – reviewed with what care?
  3. Which artifacts get committed? Specs, plans, ADRs from the workflow pyramid – which land in the repo, and where?
  4. What's your PR etiquette for agent-assisted work? The human who opens the PR owns it – the invariant, team-sized. How do you disclose agent involvement?
Show solution – an example rollout sketch
  • Ownership: AGENTS.md + style guide have a named maintainer; anyone proposes changes via PR; when an agent goes off-style for one person, the fix is a rule PR that reaches everyone.
  • Skills: live in .agents/skills/; skill PRs get the same review as code – a skill steers every agent on the team.
  • Artifacts: feature specs and plans land in docs/specs/; architecture calls become ADRs; chat transcripts don't get committed.
  • Etiquette: PR author owns every line, agent-written or not; "the AI wrote it" is never a defense in review; an AI-assisted label is welcome, accountability transfer is not.
  • Onboarding: new teammates run Lab 01's validation loop instead of getting a lecture – twenty minutes that show how the harness works and where it leaks.

Checkpoint: a named owner for the shared contracts, a review path for skills, a home for committed artifacts, and one sentence of PR etiquette your team would actually sign.

  • ✅ Your adoption plan names four concrete firsts: a convention, a skill, an autonomous task, a CI/PR-bot candidate.
  • ✅ Your model & budget strategy is written: pricing shape, a configuration per task type, and a cap for anything autonomous.
  • ✅ The evaluation and adoption sheet is filled in: comparison design, the limits table with enforcement points, and an owner per first-week practice.
  • ✅ The plan lives somewhere durable – your repo or team wiki, not a scratchpad.
  • ✅ Bonus: the team rollout is sketched – ownership, skill sharing, committed artifacts, PR etiquette.
  • ✅ You've taken the cheat sheet – the whole workshop on one page.

Closing words

The horizon moves – the bookends don't

That's the workshop. You built a harness where the repo is the prompt, a skills library, the five-part prompt, the two review passes, the pyramid, the ladder, and the blueprint – and every one of them will age. Models get smarter, instruction files get shorter, leashes get longer; re-run Lab 01's validation loop on every new model generation and prune what the model no longer needs. What won't age is the invariant you practiced in every single lab: the prompt opens the loop, and your review closes it. Everything else in your setup should change over time – that part shouldn't. Now go move your project. Thanks for building along.
